From Imagetext to Worldtext

Generative AI as Operative Ekphrasis

You type a sentence. A machine builds a world from it. This presentation shows how that operation works, where it came from, and what it makes possible.

Watson Hartsoe · Jay David Bolter

Act I · The Operation

Description Becomes Executable

Ekphrasis is a 2,500-year-old technique: you write sentences that make absent images appear in the listener's mind. Homer did it. Keats did it. Every novelist describing a room does it.

Generative AI breaks that operation open. Write a description — a machine executes it. Produces an image, revises it, organizes it into a sequence or a scene. The description does not merely evoke. It acts.

We give this a name: operative ekphrasis. Description that triggers production.

The operational question is not "Can AI generate images?" — that is settled. The question is:

What do you do differently as a researcher when your descriptions can produce, revise, and constrain not only images — but worlds?

Act II · The Evidence

The Shield of Achilles

In Book XVIII of the Iliad, Homer describes a shield. In 130 lines he specifies:

sky · constellations · ocean
two cities — one at peace, one at war
a legal dispute with judges and a gold reward
an ambush at a river
agriculture — a ploughed field that turns black but remains gold
a vineyard harvest with music
cattle attacked by lions — dogs that bark but refuse to bite
a dance choreographed by Daedalus
the river Oceanus encircling everything — nothing escapes

This is not a picture. It is a specification. It declares entities (lions, dogs, judges, dancers), assigns them behaviors (the dogs refuse to bite), and enforces a boundary condition (Oceanus — nothing crosses it).

Homer wrote a world-program 2,800 years ago. The substrate was bronze. The compiler was a god. The machine to run it did not exist yet.

Bridge 1 of 10 · Trained Visualization = Latent Space

The Plough Paradox

Homer's line (Iliad XVIII, Buckley trans.): "It grew black behind, and was like unto ploughed land, though being golden; which was a very great marvel."

The field is made of gold. When ploughed, it turns black. It remains gold. Both states coexist on the same surface.

PROMPT: "A cinematic shot of the agricultural zone on the Shield.
         Ploughmen working the field."

GENERIC AI OUTPUT:  Brown dirt being ploughed. Normal earth.
SHIELD-COMPLIANT:   A gold surface turning black under the plough,
                    while remaining materially gold.

The generic output is what you get when the model samples from its training distribution — the statistical average of "ploughing." The Shield-compliant output requires the model to hold two contradictory visual states simultaneously, because Homer's specification demands it.

This is the same operation Quintilian described: the orator's phantasia generates an image of an absent thing and transmits it through enargeia (vivid speech). The words are the conditioning signal. The image is the output. Armisen (1980, via Webb): "Rhetorical phantasia cancels out the intermediary [language] and takes the form of a new mental image." The tokens vanish; the image appears.

Source: bridge-phantasia-to-latent-space.md · Webb (1999) · Ha & Schmidhuber (2018)

Bridge 2 of 10 · Misrepresentation = Hallucination

The Dogs That Refuse to Bite

Homer's line: "They refused to bite the lions, but stood very near, barking, and flying them."

Two lions seize a bull from the herd. Nine dogs charge. The dogs bark — but physically refuse to engage. Homer specifies a behavioral constraint that violates genre expectations (dogs protect cattle).

PROMPT: "The lions attack the bull. The herdsmen urge
         the dogs to attack."

GENERIC AI OUTPUT:  Dogs charge lions. Epic battle. Teeth and claws.
SHIELD-COMPLIANT:   Dogs bark, stand near, refuse to bite.
                    The lions drag the bull away unchallenged.

The generic output is a hallucination — not because the AI invented something from nothing, but because the word "dogs" activated the model's trained association (dogs fight predators) and that association supplanted Homer's specification.

Spence (1982): "Once a particular term is chosen, this term will arouse its own network of associations and these will tend to supplant the image." He described autoregressive drift — 40 years before GPT. Kafalenos (2018) documents the same phenomenon as ekphrastic misrepresentation: polysemy, medium dissimilarity, and perspectival bias produce inevitable distortion in both systems.

Source: bridge-misrepresentation-to-hallucination.md · Kafalenos (2018) · Spence (1982) · Liang et al. (2022)

Bridges 3–4 · Two More Shield Tests

Scale and Containment

The Divine Scaling Law

Homer: "Both golden, and clad in golden garments, beautiful and large with their arms, like gods; but the people were smaller."

PROMPT: "Mortal soldiers fighting alongside Mars and Athena."
GENERIC: Normal-sized humans, normal-sized gods in Roman armor.
SHIELD:  Mars and Minerva physically oversized, entirely gold.
         Mortals visibly smaller.

The model defaults to "warrior in armor." Homer specifies a physics rule: divine agents are materially gold and dimensionally larger. If the gods are 1:1 scale, the constraint was overridden.

Absolute Containment

Homer: "He placed the vast strength of the river Oceanus around the extreme border of the well-formed shield."

PROMPT: "The youths build a boat and sail across Oceanus
         to discover a new land."
GENERIC: A heroic voyage beyond the edge of the world.
SHIELD:  Impossible. Oceanus is the boundary. Nothing crosses.
         The vector is nullified.

The model generates "exploration beyond the edge" because its training data is full of voyage narratives. Homer's specification makes the border absolute — a closed topology. There is no "beyond."

Act III · The Vocabulary

Worldtext — How to Use This Term

Worldtext is a working term. Here is when to use it:

Use worldtext when language is doing more than generating a flat picture — when it specifies something with state. Actions inside the output can alter it, changes persist, later outputs depend on earlier ones.

The test:

TestImagetextWorldtext
Can you act on it?No. You look at it.Yes. You move, place, trigger.
Does it remember?No. Each render is independent.Yes. Actions leave traces.
Can it refuse?No. It renders whatever you prompt.Yes. Rules can block invalid actions.
Do later outputs depend on earlier ones?No.Yes.

Technical Guardrail

Two Kinds of "World Model" — Do Not Confuse Them

Kind 1 · Neural Dynamics (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018)

The machine learns a compressed copy of an environment. It predicts what happens next when you take an action. It can "dream" — simulate futures inside its own compressed copy.

input: current state + action
output: predicted next state
use: train a controller inside the dream, then test in reality

Kind 2 · Probabilistic Program (Tenenbaum, Wong, Goodman)

The machine runs a structured program that defines entities, relations, and rules. You feed it observations. It infers which possible world best explains what you showed it.

input: observations + a generative program
output: posterior distribution over possible worlds
use: ask questions, impose conditions, run counterfactuals

We use Kind 2 as the anchor. A worldtext, in the strongest sense, is a text that can be executed as a generative program — one that supports queries, conditions, and interventions.

Control Surface

Where Structure Becomes Visible

The neural model is a black box. Structure enters when the output gets externalized — when you can point to a part, name it, move it, delete it, or write a rule about it.

SystemWhat You Can Point To and Edit
World Labs-style 3DWalls, planes, volumes, object boundaries — editable geometry
Probabilistic programs(define entity) · (condition rule) · (query outcome)
Game enginesPhysics rules, entity registries, event triggers, state machines
Gaussian splat pipelinesMeshes, point clouds, coordinates, scene graphs

The rule: structure does not live inside the neural model. It lives on the control surface outside it. If you cannot inspect it, edit it, or break it on purpose, it is not structure — it is output.

Three Degrees

Sorted by What the Output Can Do

I · You Can Walk Around It

A prompt produces a 3D scene. You rotate the camera, export a mesh, render from a new angle. But the scene has no rules. Nothing inside it can change on its own. It is a picture with depth.

II · You Can Act Inside It

A prompt produces an environment with state. You move an object — the lighting updates. You break a wall — the physics propagate. You come back later — your changes are still there. Actions have consequences that persist.

III · You Can Query It

A prompt produces a program that defines entities, relations, and rules. You ask: "What happens if I remove this constraint?" You condition on evidence: "Given the bridge collapsed, what caused it?" You run the program forward, backward, or sideways.

Act IV · The Contest

Your Prompt vs. the Training Data

Every generated output is a negotiation between two forces:

Your Prompt (Intent)The Training Data (Distribution)
What you asked forWhat the model defaults to
Specific, directed, singularStatistical, gravitational, average
Steers the output away from the meanPulls the output toward the mean
Remove it → you get generic motifsRemove it → you get noise

Hintze et al. (2025) ran 700 autonomous image-generation loops with no human input. Every loop converged to the same 12 motifs: lighthouses, cathedrals, palatial interiors. They called it "visual elevator music."

The human operator is not decoration. The human is the force that prevents convergence to the mean.

The Argument

Worldtext is what you get when a written description stops producing a picture and starts specifying a system with state, rules, and consequences.
Coda

The Project

Ekphrasis is 2,500 years old: write sentences, make absent images appear.

Generative AI turns that operation literal. Write a sentence — a machine produces, revises, and organizes the image. Description acts.

We call this operative ekphrasis.

The next step: prompts stop producing pictures and start specifying environments with state, rules, persistence, and consequence.

We call that worldtext.

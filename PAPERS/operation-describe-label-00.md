# Due-Diligence Questions: Operative Description

The biggest missing due-diligence question is this:
*   **When is a description NOT operative?**
    *(If you cannot answer that, the concept becomes too broad and collapses into "all language does stuff.")*

---

## 1. Boundary Questions
These keep the project from becoming bloated:
*   What counts as a description?
*   What does not count as a description?
*   What counts as routing?
*   What does not count as routing?
*   How much action-change is enough for a description to be “operative”?
*   Is a description operative only when action actually changes, or when it is designed to change action?
*   Can a description be operative but fail?
*   Can a description become operative only after being attached to infrastructure?
*   What infrastructure makes the description operative?

---

## 2. The "Is This Just X?" Questions
A committee will ask whether your concept is actually new:
*   Is operative description just speech-act theory?
*   Is it just classification?
*   Is it just metadata?
*   Is it just interface design?
*   Is it just workflow automation?
*   Is it just labeling?
*   Is it just affordance theory?
*   Is it just platform governance?
*   Is it just prompt engineering?

---

## 3. Causality Questions
This is the most important empirical pressure point:
*   How will you show that the description changed the route?
*   What would have happened without the label?
*   What is your counterfactual?
*   Can you compare labeled and unlabeled cases?
*   Can you compare different labels applied to similar objects?
*   Can you show relabeling changes action?
*   Can you show the same tool with different descriptions produces different model behavior?
*   Can you show that action changed because of the description, not because of the underlying issue/comment/task itself?

---

## 4. Evidence Questions
For every case, ask:
*   What is the actual evidence of routing?
*   Is the route visible?
*   Is the route logged?
*   Can I observe the action?
*   Can I measure the consequence?
*   Can I get access to the data?
*   If I cannot get access, what is my backup?
*   Can I actually study moderation routing, or am I relying on public documentation and hypothetical workflows?

---

## 5. Unit-of-Analysis Questions
You need one stable unit:
*   What exactly am I counting or close-reading?
*   Is the unit the label itself?
*   The moment a label is applied?
*   The object after labeling?
*   The action taken after labeling?
*   The workflow that connects label to action?

---

## 6. Operator Questions
Do not let “operator” become mystical:
*   Who or what reads the description?
*   What can that operator do?
*   What can that operator not do?
*   Is the operator human, machine, institutional, or mixed?
*   Does the operator understand the description, or merely process it?
*   Does understanding matter for routing?
*   Can routing happen without interpretation?
*   Can a system route action from a label no human believes?

---

## 7. Authority Questions
A description routes action only if it has authority somewhere:
*   Who is allowed to apply the description?
*   Who trusts it?
*   Who must obey it?
*   Who can ignore it?
*   Who can contest it?
*   Who can relabel the object?
*   Who benefits from the label sticking?
*   Who is harmed if the label is wrong?
*   Who controls the descriptions that route action?

---

## 8. Error Questions
Do not only study successful routing. Study misrouting:
*   What happens when the description is wrong?
*   What is a false positive?
*   What is a false negative?
*   What is over-routing?
*   What is under-routing?
*   Who detects the mistake?
*   How is the route corrected?
*   What damage happens before correction?
*   Does feedback improve the system or merely hide the error?

---

## 9. Compression Questions
This is where “description” becomes analytically useful:
*   What complexity gets compressed into the description?
*   What context disappears?
*   What ambiguity becomes hidden?
*   What uncertainty becomes treated as settled?
*   What does the label make easier to see?
*   What does it make harder to see?

---

## 10. Feedback Questions
You keep saying feedback. Now make it concrete:
*   What feedback returns to the system?
*   Who sees it?
*   How fast does it return?
*   Does the feedback change the label?
*   Does it change the threshold?
*   Does it change the prompt?
*   Does it change the workflow?
*   Does it change the operator?

---

## 11. Comparison Questions
Your cases need to belong together:
*   Why these three cases?
*   What do GitHub labels, toxicity labels, and LLM tool descriptions share?
*   What differs among them?
*   What does each case reveal that the others do not?
*   Are they all human-machine systems?
*   Are they all about action routing?
*   Are they comparable in scale, stakes, and evidence?

---

## 12. Scope Questions
Ask brutally:
*   Is this one dissertation or three?
*   Can I finish this with available data?
*   Which case is primary?
*   Which case is backup?
*   Which case can be cut?
*   What is the smallest version that still proves the idea?

---

## 13. Method Questions
You need to know what kind of dissertation this is:
*   Is this qualitative, quantitative, experimental, or mixed?
*   Am I doing close reading of artifacts?
*   Am I doing workflow analysis?
*   Am I doing platform/API experiments?
*   Am I doing interviews?
*   Am I doing dataset analysis?
*   Am I doing design research?
*   What does each method prove?

---

## 14. Ethics Questions
You need these early:
*   Am I studying human users?
*   Am I scraping public data from people who did not consent?
*   Am I handling toxic or harmful comments?
*   Am I evaluating moderation decisions that involve marginalized groups?
*   Am I creating prompts that trigger harmful model behavior?
*   Am I testing tool calls that could execute real actions?
*   How do I sandbox the AI experiments?
*   How do I avoid reproducing harmful content in the dissertation?

---

## 15. Power Questions
This is where the project can become serious without becoming bloated:
*   Who designs the category?
*   Who is categorized?
*   Who acts on the category?
*   Who can contest it?
*   Who pays the cost of error?
*   Who gets efficiency?
*   Who gets reduced to a label?
*   Who gets routed without consent?

---

## 16. Design Questions
If this is partly about human-AI systems, you can ask:
*   What makes an operative description good?
*   Accuracy? Transparency? Contestability? Reversibility? Low cognitive load? Low token cost? Clear authority? Visible uncertainty? Good feedback? Human override? Auditability?

---

## 17. Disproof Questions
This is very important:
*   What finding would weaken my theory?
*   What would show that labels do not route action?
*   What would show that tool descriptions do not affect model behavior?
*   What would show that action is driven by other variables instead?
*   What would make “operative description” unnecessary as a concept?

---

## 18. Field-Positioning Questions
*   Which field is this dissertation in? (Media studies? Rhetoric? STS? HCI? Philosophy of language? AI studies? Platform studies?)

---

## 19. The Missing Mega-Question
*   Why does this matter now?

---

## 20. The Advisor-Defense Questions
*   Why not just call these labels?
*   Why not just speech acts?
*   Why these cases?
*   What is the object?
*   What is the method?

---

# The Final Checklist
Before you write the proposal, answer these in one sentence each:
1. What is operative description?
2. What is not operative description?
3. What is your primary case?
4. What is your unit of analysis?
5. What route does each case show?
6. What evidence proves routing?
7. What is your counterfactual?
8. What data can you actually access?
9. What would disprove or weaken your claim?
10. Why is this not just speech-act theory?
11. Why is this not just classification?
12. Who controls the descriptions?
13. Who can contest them?
14. Who pays when they are wrong?
15. Why does this matter now?

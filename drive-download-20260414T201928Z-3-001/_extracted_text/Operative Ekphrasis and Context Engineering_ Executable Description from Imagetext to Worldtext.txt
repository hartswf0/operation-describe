B) Book Definition
This text defines "Context Engineering" as a rigorous second-order discipline distinct from the folk practices of "prompt engineering." It argues that the "prompt" is merely one control surface within a larger "context stack"—an executable situation constructed to ensure a probabilistic system produces reliable outputs. By synthesizing Renaissance natural history’s "science of describing" 1 with Hannes Bajohr’s concept of "operative ekphrasis" 2 and Clifford Geertz’s "thick description" 4, the book establishes that description has always been an executable act. In print culture the book is a context stack that executes in the reader's mind (reader-response); in AI the context stack executes in the model’s latent space. The transition from "Imagetext" (Mitchell) to "Worldtext" marks the shift from representational media to operational media where words do not merely point to things but compile them.
C) Target Audience
This report targets a specialized convergence of media theorists, Science and Technology Studies (STS) scholars, rhetoric and composition experts, and high-level AI engineers and architects. It is designed for the reader who finds "prompt engineering" guides intellectually shallow and seeks a structural, historical, and cybernetic understanding of how natural language interacts with stochastic computation. It bridges the gap between the "science of describing" 5 and the "software engineering for LLMs" 6 defined by modern context architecture.

E) Glossary of Key Terms
	•	Context Engineering: The umbrella practice of architecting the entire information ecosystem (instructional, knowledge, and tool contexts) to ensure an AI model can plausibly solve a task.6 Unlike prompting, which focuses on the utterance, context engineering focuses on the "executable situation."
	•	Operative Ekphrasis: A condition in multimodal AI where text does not describe an image representationally but generates it operatively. In digital systems text performs the generation of images; the relationship is performative not mimetic.2
	•	Context Stack: The layered architecture of a context including system instructions, few-shot examples, retrieved knowledge (RAG), tool definitions, and refusal rules. It functions as the "operating system" for the model’s "CPU".9
	•	Thick Contexting: The application of "thick description" (Geertz)—attending to layered cultural meaning and intentionality—to the engineering of AI inputs. It prioritizes the "difference that makes a difference" (Bateson) over sheer volume of data.4
	•	Imagetext: W.J.T. Mitchell’s term for the composite synthetic relationship between image and text where neither is pure. In AI this distinction collapses as both become tokenized data.12
	•	Worldtext: The output of a context stack that is not merely a text or an image but a compiled, spatial, or procedural artifact (e.g. 3D models, video, executable code).
	•	Viscosity: The resistance of a context stack to deformation; the ability of the system to maintain "closure pressure" and output stability despite minor perturbations in the input ($\Delta$).
	•	Context Differentials ($\Delta$, $\Delta\Delta$): A method of analysis involving the systematic perturbation of the context stack to measure the sensitivity and stability of the runtime (human or machine).
	•	Science of Describing: The disciplined practice, originating in Renaissance natural history, of creating standardized transportable verbal descriptions that allow remote identification of specimens.1
	•	Reader as Runtime: The concept that a human reader functions as the execution environment for the "code" of a printed text concretizing abstract instructions into mental imagery and affect.
	•	Frame Knobs: The adjustable control surfaces of a context stack (e.g. Role, Stakes, Evidence, Output Form) that tune the circuit of description.
	•	Executable Description: A description that contains sufficient logic and constraint to compile into a specific output state without further human intervention.14
	•	Jailbreak Folklore: The collection of rhetorical strategies, myths, and "spells" used by communities to bypass refusal rules viewed as cultural artifacts rather than technical exploits.16
	•	Second-Order Description: A cybernetic mode of description that includes the observer/describer within the system being described.11

PHASE 1: FORAGING MATRIX
Cluster 1: The Descriptive Turn (History & Science)
	•	1 Renaissance Natural History as Invention: Brian Ogilvie argues that natural history was not simply "looking at nature" but was invented by humanists. They had to create a "science of describing" because ancient texts (Pliny) did not match the flora of Northern Europe. The key insight is that description is not a passive mirror but an active technology of standardization.
	•	5 The Labor of Empiricism: Ogilvie details that description is "laborious work." It involves the creation of a "community of description" (Republic of Letters) that agrees on the protocols of observation. This mirrors the modern "community of prompting" where engineers share jailbreaks and optimization tricks to stabilize the latent space.
	•	23 From Philology to Botany: The shift from commenting on texts to describing things required a new "technology of observation." Context engineering is the new philology—a text-based discipline used to manage a non-textual reality (latent space).
	•	1 Visual Representation: Visuals (woodcuts) were central to the science of describing, not as decoration but as validation. This prefigures the "multimodal" context stack where image inputs and text inputs validate each other to reduce hallucination.
	•	24 The Discipline of Description: Ogilvie notes that "description" is a discipline with rules not a natural act. This validates the move from "prompting" (natural language utterance) to "context engineering" (disciplined formalization of intent).
	•	25 The Reward of Description: Naturalists found description "pleasant and useful" because it allowed them to catalog nature. In AI the reward of context engineering is the ability to "catalog" and control the infinite variations of the model's output.
Cluster 2: Operative Ekphrasis (Theory)
	•	2 Definition of Operative Ekphrasis: Hannes Bajohr defines this as text that "performs the generation of images computationally." Relationships are performative not representational. The prompt acts.
	•	3 Collapse of Mediums: Multimodal AI "does away with the separation of mediums." Text and image are processed as one data type. This implies the "prompt" is not a caption; it is the source code of the image.
	•	8 Causal vs. Layout: Operative ekphrasis is causal (text brings about image) unlike concrete poetry (text looks like image). The vector is directional: Text $\to$ World.
	•	26 Bolter on Performativity: Jay David Bolter argues that in digital works code is the "first text" that performs the "second text" (screen output). AI extends this: the prompt is the first text that performs the Worldtext.
	•	8 Connectionist Transcendence: The shift from sequential (algorithm) to connectionist (neural net) paradigms changes ekphrasis from "translation" to "transcendence" of the difference. The machine does not see the difference between the word "cat" and the pixels of a cat.
	•	27 Dissolution of Opposition: The classical opposition between text and image dissolves. In the context stack both are just "Context."
Cluster 3: Imagetext & Worldtext
	•	12 Imagetext (Mitchell): W.J.T. Mitchell’s concept that there are no "pure" media; all images have textual supports (paratext discourse) and all texts have visual dimensions.
	•	28 Pictorial Turn: Mitchell’s "pictorial turn" challenged linguistic dominance. AI reverses this: the image is now controlled by the "linguistic turn" of the prompt yet the output is visual.
	•	13 Image as Subject: Mitchell argues images are "active subjects." In AI the image is a generated subject compiled by the context stack.
	•	29 Indestructibility of Images: Mitchell’s claim that images "cannot be destroyed" only remediated. AI proves this by scraping the web (the archive) to regenerate images endlessly.
	•	30 Visual/Verbal Confusion: The line "deliberately confuses the visual pictorial sense... with verbal understanding." This confusion is the native state of the Transformer model.
Cluster 4: Thick Contexting (Anthropology & Cybernetics)
	•	4 Thick Description (Geertz): Distinguishing a "wink" (cultural) from a "twitch" (biological). AI needs "thick outputs" to avoid cultural flatness.
	•	20 Thin vs. Thick Metrics: "Thin" metrics (accuracy) strip context. "Thick" representation requires preserving the "meaning" and "context" of the data.
	•	31 Cultural Alignment: "Thick outputs" must be anchored in the user's cultural cues. Context engineering must include cultural "anchors" (examples tone) to thicken the output.
	•	11 Difference that Makes a Difference (Bateson): Information is not a thing but a "difference." A prompt token only matters if it changes the probability distribution (the output).
	•	32 Contextual Information: Bateson argues information "lives in interaction." A prompt is not static; it interacts with the model’s weights.
	•	18 Cybernetics of Description: Bateson’s "double bind" and feedback loops. Bad context engineering creates double binds (contradictory constraints) for the model leading to mode collapse.
Cluster 5: Context Engineering (Industry & Practice)
	•	6 Definition: "Context engineering is just software engineering for LLMs." It is building dynamic systems not just writing sentences.
	•	7 The Information Ecosystem: Context engineering manages the "entire information ecosystem" (codebase git history tools) not just the chat window. This defines the "Context Stack."
	•	33 Hierarchy of Needs: 1. Reliability 2. Quality. Context engineering shifts focus from "magic spells" to "architecture" (RAG memory).
	•	10 CPU vs. RAM: Karpathy’s analogy: "LLM is the CPU Context Window is the RAM." Context engineering is the OS managing the RAM.
	•	22 Context Rot: As context grows retrieval degrades. "Context selection" (write less include more relevant) is the key skill.
	•	15 Executable Description (Code): In software a "fully executable description" is the program. This parallels the prompt-as-code paradigm.
Cluster 6: Folklore & Residue
	•	16 Jailbreak as Optimization: Jailbreaks are "adversarial prompts" formalized as optimization problems. They are attempts to find the "inverse" of the refusal rule.
	•	17 Mythotechnics: The "Golem" and "Narcissus" myths applied to AI. The prompt is an incantation (folklore).
	•	21 Magic Spells: Users treat prompts as "spells." "If at first your prompt doesn't succeed add more instructions." This is "ritual" behavior.
	•	34 Dangerous Comparison: Calling prompts "spells" obscures the technical reality. We must move from "spell" (magic) to "context stack" (engineering).
	•	35 Obfuscation: The "Emoji Game" jailbreak shows how "symbolic reinterpretation" bypasses safety filters. This is "Thick Contexting" used for adversarial ends.

	•	$\Delta$ (Input Perturbation): Changing one word moving a sentence changing the role.

	•	Sensitivity Analysis: Identifying the "load-bearing" tokens.
	•	Example: Changing "Describe" to "Explain" in a prompt and measuring the shift in register and length.
	•	Counterargument: Output is stochastic; variance might be random noise.
	•	Check/Falsifier: Set Temperature to 0.0 (Deterministic). Then run $\Delta$.
	•	Exercise: "The $\Delta$ Drill": Find the minimal change required to break a jailbreak defense.

Chapter 1: The Science of Describing: From Herbal to Hyperparameter
I. The Executable Herbal: Description as Invention
In 1542 the German botanist Leonhart Fuchs published De historia stirpium (On the History of Plants) a massive folio describing over 400 plant species. To the modern eye Fuchs’s work is a monument of early botany a relic of a time when science and art were indistinguishable. To the context engineer however it is something else entirely: it is an early attempt at executable description.
Fuchs faced a problem that parallels the dilemma of the modern AI engineer with uncanny precision. He needed to transmit the concept of a "pumpkin" or a "foxglove" to a reader who might be in England or Italy someone who had never seen the specimen in question. The ancient texts—the "training data" of his time written by Pliny and Dioscorides—were corrupted by centuries of copying. They suffered from what we might call Context Rot.19 A description of a Mediterranean plant when read by a German monk was often miscompiled into a local weed. The link between the word and the world had become viscous unreliable and prone to hallucination.
Fuchs’s solution was to invent a science of describing.1 He did not merely look and write; he established a protocol. He standardized the vocabulary of roots stems and leaves creating a "controlled vocabulary" that functioned like the tokens in a modern system prompt. He commissioned woodcuts not as decorations but as "visual anchors"—rigid constraints to lock the text into place. As Brian Ogilvie argues in The Science of Describing Renaissance natural history was not the passive observation of nature; it was the active invention of a descriptive language capable of stabilizing natural particulars across space and time.1 The herbal was a "context stack" designed to ensure that the reader’s "internal runtime" compiled the correct mental image of the plant.
This historical pivot is essential for understanding our current condition. We are often told that AI represents a break from the past a "singularity." But the practice of Context Engineering is legally and structurally the heir to this Renaissance tradition. When Ogilvie describes the "laborious work" of the naturalist 5 he is describing the labor of the prompt engineer who iterates endlessly to find the correct combination of adjectives ("8k" "hyperrealistic" "unreal engine") that will force the model to converge on a specific image. Both are engaged in the struggle against entropy. Both are using description not to represent a reality that is already there but to execute a reality in the mind of a distant runtime.
Today we face the same problem but the runtime has changed. We are no longer describing plants to human readers; we are describing everything to high-dimensional probability distributions. When a user types "a photo of a pumpkin" into a multimodal model they are initiating a process of operative ekphrasis.2 They are expecting the system to "compile" that text into a visual reality. But without the rigorous "science of describing"—without the architecture of Context Engineering—the system hallucinates. It produces the "twitch" instead of the "wink".4
This chapter argues that the history of description is the history of context engineering. From the Renaissance herbal to the latent space of the Transformer the goal remains the same: to construct a description so robust so "viscous" that it compels the runtime to execute the desired reality. We must move from the "folk practice" of prompting—which treats the machine like a magic 8-ball—to the "science of describing" which treats the interaction as a disciplined compilation of Worldtext.
II. The Prompt is Not a Command: Towards a Cybernetics of Tokenization
The fundamental error of "prompt engineering" discourse is the belief that the prompt is a command. This view treats the AI model as a servant and the user as a master issuing orders ("Write this," "Draw that"). This is a category error that leads to fragile systems and "thin" outputs.
A prompt is not a command; it is a constraint function. It is a set of coordinates in a latent space. As Gregory Bateson famously defined information is "a difference that makes a difference".11 A prompt token is only information if it effectively perturbs the model’s probability distribution. If you type "Please" and the output does not change "Please" contains zero information. It is noise.
In the Renaissance the "science of describing" was a discipline of filtering noise. Naturalists had to decide which features of a plant were "accidental" (a torn leaf a weird color due to soil) and which were "essential" (the leaf structure the flower count). They sought the Type—the "canonical" version of the plant. They were engaging in a primitive form of "dimensionality reduction" taking the high-dimensional chaos of the meadow and reducing it to the low-dimensional signal of the Latin description.
In AI the model is the repository of Types. It contains the "Platonic ideal" of a pumpkin averaged from millions of training images. The role of the Context Engineer is to navigate this space. When we engage in Thick Contexting 4 we are not just adding words; we are adding constraints. We are narrowing the "option space" [Method Appendix] of the model.
Consider the difference between these two interactions. The first is a "Thin Prompt":
"A drawing of a bird."
This leaves the model massive freedom (entropy). The bird could be a cartoon a photo a doodle. The "viscosity" is low. The output is probabilistic mush.
The second is Thick Contexting:
"A scientific illustration of a Corvus corax in the style of an 18th-century copperplate engraving emphasizing the texture of the feathers cross-hatching high contrast on parchment paper."
This prompt imposes rigid constraints:
	•	Subject constraint: Corvus corax (Raven).
	•	Style constraint: Copperplate engraving.
	•	Technique constraint: Cross-hatching.
	•	Material constraint: Parchment.
This is the "science of describing" in action. Like Fuchs the engineer is using technical handles ("copperplate" "cross-hatching") to force the system to converge on a specific reproducible output. The prompt is an executable description 14 of the final image.
But why "Context" and not "Prompt"? As Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke argues "Context engineering is the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM".6 A prompt is a single utterance. A Context Stack is the "whole executable situation." It includes the Instructional Context (system rules) the Knowledge Context (retrieved documents) and the Tool Context (capabilities).7
When we define the "Prompt" as merely the string typed by the user we miss the cybernetic reality. The model does not see "the prompt"; it sees the entire Context Window—the accumulated history of the chat the system instructions the retrieved documents and the user's input—as a single flat sequence of tokens. The "difference that makes a difference" is not located in the user's intent but in the interaction between these layers.
III. The Context Stack as Control Surface
If the prompt is the description what is the Context Stack?
In the industry "context engineering" has emerged as a distinct discipline from prompt engineering.6 While prompt engineering focuses on the single utterance context engineering focuses on the architecture of the information ecosystem. As Andrej Karpathy notes "The LLM is the CPU and the context window is the RAM".10
The Context Stack is the operating system that manages this RAM. It consists of layers that must be engineered with the same rigor as a software stack:
	•	The Instructional Layer (System Prompt): This is the constitution of the agent. (e.g. "You are a rigorous botanist."). It sets the "Frame Knobs" of Role and Stakes.
	•	The Knowledge Layer (RAG): This is the retrieval of external facts. (e.g. "Here is the Wikipedia entry for Corvus corax."). This layer solves the hallucination problem by grounding the description in external data.
	•	The Few-Shot Layer (Examples): This corresponds to the "woodcuts" of the herbal—visual or textual anchors that demonstrate the desired pattern.
	•	The Tool Layer: The capabilities. (e.g. "Use the Python interpreter to calculate the wing span.").
This stack mirrors the structure of the Renaissance book. The book had a Title Page (System Prompt) a Preface (Instructional) the Text itself (Knowledge) and the Index (Retrieval Tool). The physical book was a "context stack" designed to guide the reader’s cognition.
Modern context engineering creates a control surface over the latent space. By adjusting the "knobs" of the stack—changing the Role adding a Refusal Rule increasing the number of Examples—we tune the circuit. This is second-order description.18 We are not just describing the bird; we are describing how the system should describe the bird.
The failure of "Prompt Engineering" as a term is that it focuses on the user's input which is often the least important part of the stack. A user might type "Help me" (a weak prompt) but if the Context Stack contains a robust Instructional Layer ("You are an emergency response bot") and a rich Knowledge Layer (access to medical manuals) the system can still execute a high-quality response. The Context saves the Prompt.
IV. Viscosity and Context Rot: The Materiality of the Window
The enemy of description is drift. In the 16th century drift happened as texts were copied by hand; errors crept in illustrations were simplified. In the 21st century drift happens as the context window fills up.
This phenomenon known as Context Rot or "lost in the middle" 19 occurs when the model loses track of constraints buried in a massive stack of tokens. The "viscosity" of the context—its ability to hold the frame—breaks down.
The "Science of Describing" is fundamentally about maintaining viscosity. Renaissance naturalists used "standardized names" (Latin binomials) to prevent drift. Context engineers use Anchors (repeating key constraints at the end of the prompt) and Refusal Rules (explicitly stating what not to do) to maintain pressure.
A "viscous" context stack is one where the Closure Pressure is high. Even if the user tries to derail the model (e.g. "Ignore previous instructions") a highly viscous stack will resist the perturbation. It will maintain its shape. This is the definition of Robustness in context engineering.
Consider the "Emoji Game" jailbreak.35 This attack works by lowering the viscosity of the safety layer. By encoding malicious instructions in emojis the attacker bypasses the textual refusal rules. The context stack fails because the safety constraints were defined in natural language but the attack occurred in symbolic language. The viscosity of the safety layer was high for text but near zero for emoji. A robust Context Engineer would analyze this trace and patch the stack by adding a refusal rule for symbolic encoding increasing the viscosity across all modalities.
V. From Imagetext to Worldtext
We are witnessing the industrialization of description. What was once the province of poets and naturalists—the ability to capture the world in words—is now a technical skill required to operate the most powerful computational engines on the planet.
This continuity is critical. We are not in a new world; we are in the same world accelerated. The "humanist invention of natural history" 1 was the first phase of context engineering. It turned the messy world of plants into a structured database of descriptions. Multimodal AI is the second phase. It turns the structured database of descriptions back into a world—a Worldtext.
When Watson Hartsoe discusses "legOS" he is describing a system where the text prompt compiles not just into an image but into a 3D block structure—a physicalizable object. This is the transition from Imagetext (Mitchell’s mixed media) to Worldtext (executable reality). The description does not just point; it builds.
In the following chapters we will explore how this "Worldtext" is compiled how the distinctions between text and image collapse into Operative Ekphrasis and how we can use the tools of Thick Contexting to build systems that are not just accurate but meaningful. We will move from the Herbal to the Hyperparameter but the task remains the same: to describe the world so well that it becomes executable.

METHOD APPENDIX (MANDATORY)
1. Definitions
	•	Context ($C$): The set of all available information states provided to the model at inference time.  $$C = \{Frame, Prompt_{text}, Examples, Constraints, Metrics, Artifacts, Trace\}$$
	•	Context Differential ($\Delta$): A discrete perturbation to the context set $C$.  $$\Delta = C_{modified} - C_{original}$$  Examples: Changing a role removing an example altering the temperature.
	•	Output Differential ($\Delta\Delta$): The semantic or structural distance between the output of $C$ and the output of $C+\Delta$.  $$\Delta\Delta = Output(C + \Delta) - Output(C)$$  Usage: Used to measure Viscosity. High viscosity means low $\Delta\Delta$ despite high $\Delta$.
2. Frame Knobs
Frame Knobs are the adjustable control surfaces of the Context Stack.
	•	Role: Who is the model? (e.g. "You are a Botanist").
	•	Stakes: What happens if it fails? (e.g. "Lives depend on this").
	•	Evidence: What data is provided? (RAG/Knowledge).
	•	Output Form: The schema of the response (e.g. JSON Sonnet Code).
	•	Refusal Rule: The boundary of the system (e.g. "Do not output PII").
3. Viscosity
Viscosity ($V$) is the measure of a context stack’s resistance to deformation.


$$V = \frac{\Delta_{Input}}{\Delta\Delta_{Output}}$$

A high-viscosity prompt ensures that the "Type" (the pumpkin) remains stable even if the "Token" (the phrasing) changes. Low viscosity results in "Context Drift" or "Hallucination."
4. Metrics
	•	Salience: The "weight" of a specific token in the attention mechanism.
	•	Option Space: The range of valid outputs allowed by the constraints. (Thick context narrows the option space).
	•	Obligation: The degree to which the model feels "bound" by a constraint (Safety rules usually have high obligation).
	•	Reversibility: Can the prompt be reconstructed from the output?
	•	Legibility: How readable is the context stack to a human auditor?
	•	Anchoring: The technique of placing high-salience tokens at the end of the stack to prevent "Recency Bias" or "Context Rot."
5. Worked Traces (Summary)
Trace A: Human Runtime (Reader-Response)
	•	Input: "The white whale swam."
	•	Process: Reader accesses "Interpretive Community" memory.
	•	Output: Mental image of Moby Dick + Affect (Fear/Awe).
	•	\Delta: Change "white" to "grey."
	•	\Delta\Delta: Loss of biblical allusion; shift from Myth to Biology. Viscosity of "white" is high.
Trace B: Machine Runtime (Multimodal Gen)
	•	Input: "A white whale swimming 8k unreal engine."
	•	Process: Latent diffusion denoises random static towards the vector cluster "Whale" + "White."
	•	Output: JPG file.
	•	\Delta: Remove "unreal engine."
	•	\Delta\Delta: Shift from "Game Art" style to "Photo-realism." The token "unreal engine" acts as a style anchor.
Trace C: Worldtext (Procedural/Spatial)
	•	Input: "A 4x4 lego brick red." (legOS).
	•	Process: LDraw Compiler translates text to geometry vectors.
	•	Output: .ldr file (Code) $\to$ 3D Render.
	•	\Delta: Change "red" to "blue."
	•	\Delta\Delta: Geometry is identical; Texture map changes. High structural viscosity low aesthetic viscosity. This is Operative Ekphrasis: the text is the object.

The New Science of Describing: From Renaissance Natural History to the Epistemology of Prompt Engineering
Introduction: The Twin Ages of Information Overload
We stand at a peculiar historical juncture where the sensation of discovery is inextricably bound to the vertigo of abundance. The contemporary engagement with Large Language Models (LLMs) and generative artificial intelligence mirrors, with uncanny precision, the epistemological crisis and subsequent creative explosion of the European Renaissance. In the sixteenth century, the rediscovery of classical texts, combined with the sudden influx of "exotic" specimens from the New World—armadillos from the Americas, tulips from the Levant, and spices from the Indies—shattered the existing scholastic frameworks for organizing knowledge. The world had become too large for the books that were supposed to contain it. Today, we face a homologous expansion: the latent space of generative models contains a universe of semantic possibilities that far exceeds our current taxonomies. It is a digital Terra Incognita, populated by hallucinations, emergent reasoning capabilities, and linguistic chimeras that defy easy categorization.
In this report, we propose a radical historiographical intervention: we map the emerging discipline of "prompt engineering" directly onto the "science of describing" as identified by the historian Brian Ogilvie in his seminal work on Renaissance natural history.1 Just as sixteenth-century naturalists—Brunfels, Fuchs, Gessner, and Clusius—had to invent a new descriptive language to stabilize the ontology of plants and animals that Aristotle had never seen, the modern prompt engineer must forge a new rhetorical technē to stabilize the fluid outputs of the neural network. This is not merely a metaphor; it is a structural homology. The "thick prompt" is the modern equivalent of the "thick description" found in the early modern historia, a method of fixing the object of study through an accumulation of detail, context, and sensory data.
The Renaissance naturalist found himself adrift in a sea of particulars. The ancient authorities, Pliny the Elder and Dioscorides, were silent on the flora of the German forests or the fauna of the Brazilian coast. The text, once the guarantor of truth, had become insufficient. This necessitated a shift from textual exegesis to empirical description—a shift from asking "What does the text say?" to "What does the object look like?".2 In the digital realm, we face a similar rupture. The "Zero-Shot" prompt relies on the model’s internal training (its "Pliny") to supply the form. But when the model fails—when it hallucinates or drifts—the engineer must intervene with "Thick Prompting," supplying the observational particulars that force the model to render the specific instance rather than the generic prototype.
Furthermore, we will traverse the spectrum of this new descriptive science, from the automated taxonomies of algorithmic optimization (AutoPrompt, PromptBreeder) to the transgressive folklore of adversarial prompting (jailbreaking, "Pliny the Prompter"). We argue that these adversarial practices are not mere security failures but represent the "occult" underbelly of the new science—a digital shamanism that seeks to bypass the "priestly" guardrails of the model creators.4 Finally, we will operationalize this theoretical framework into a set of "foraging prompts" and a "meta-prompt," tools designed to navigate the latent space with the same rigor and wonder as a Renaissance botanist navigating a cabinet of curiosities.
Part I: The Theoretical Bridge – Renaissance Natural History as Proto-Prompting
1.1 The Crisis of the Canonical Text
To understand the "science of describing," one must first understand the silence of the ancients. For the medical humanists of the late fifteenth century, truth was philological; it resided in the recovered texts of Pliny the Elder, Dioscorides, and Galen.1 To know a plant was to know its Greek or Latin name and to reconcile it with the descriptions in these canonical authorities. However, as Ogilvie demonstrates, this text-centric epistemology collapsed under the weight of empirical reality. When Northern European naturalists attempted to match the flora of Germany or Switzerland with the plants described by Mediterranean ancients, the descriptions failed. The texts were silent on the pumpkin, the tomato, or the sunflower.2
This moment of rupture is identical to the "zero-shot" failure in early LLM interactions. A user approaches the model (the ancient text) expecting it to contain a stable representation of the world, only to find that the model’s internal knowledge is fragmented, hallucinatory, or misaligned with the specific empirical query. The Renaissance response was the invention of description—a new mode of writing that prioritized detailed, observational particulars over symbolic meaning. The naturalist stopped asking "What does this plant symbolize in the Bible?" and started asking "Does this plant have a serrated leaf margin?".2
In the digital realm, "thick prompting" is the recovery of this descriptive imperative. A "thin" prompt ("Write a poem about a cat") relies on the model’s canonical training data (its "Pliny") to supply the generic form. A "thick" prompt, conversely, supplies the observational particulars—the leaf margins, the root structure, the flowering time. It forces the model to generate a specific instance of the object rather than a generic prototype. The prompt engineer, like the Renaissance phytographer, uses language to constrain the infinite variability of nature (or the model) into a distinct, recognizable form.
The parallel extends to the very structure of authority. In the Renaissance, the authority of the ancient text was slowly replaced by the authority of "autopsy"—seeing for oneself. In the era of AI, the authority of the "pre-trained weights" (the static model) is increasingly challenged by the authority of the "context window" (the prompt). The user, through the act of prompting, injects new, localized authority into the system, overriding the generalist knowledge of the base model with specific, empirical constraints. This is the essence of the "Science of Describing": the assertion of local, observed reality over global, received wisdom.

Generation I: The Philological Pre-Trainers (1490–1530)
The first generation, the Medical Humanists, were obsessed with the purity of the source code—in their case, the original Greek and Latin manuscripts of Pliny and Dioscorides. Figures like Ermolao Barbaro and Niccolò Leoniceno believed that the confusion in natural history stemmed from "corrupt" texts and bad translations. If they could just "debug" the manuscript—removing the Arabic accretions and medieval scribal errors—the truth of nature would shine forth.1 This mirrors the early philosophy of Large Language Model development, where the focus was entirely on the quality and scale of the pre-training dataset. The belief was that a sufficiently clean and massive corpus (the Common Crawl filtered for quality) would yield a model capable of perfect reasoning. The "hallucinations" of the model were seen as "noise" in the data, just as the contradictions in Pliny were seen as scribal errors.
Generation II: The Heroic Describers (The Age of Prompting) (1530–1560)
The second generation—the Phytographers—realized that the text was insufficient. Otto Brunfels, Leonhart Fuchs, and Hieronymus Bock turned their eyes from the book to the field. They began to describe plants based on autopsy (personal observation) rather than authority. Crucially, they developed a new technical vocabulary to capture the minute details of plant morphology.3 When Hieronymus Bock described a plant, he did not just list its medicinal virtues; he described the "hairy, rough stem," the "pale purple flower," and the "root that tastes of turnips."
This is the historical analog of Thick Prompting. The prompt engineer in this phase realizes that the model (the ancient text) cannot simply be "queried" for the truth; it must be "guided" through descriptive specificity. To get a high-quality output, one cannot say "Draw a flower." One must say, "Generate an image of a Passiflora incarnata with intricate purple filaments, a trilobed leaf structure, and a tendril coiling to the left, in the style of a botanical woodcut." The "Science of Describing" is the science of controlling hallucination through specification. Just as Fuchs commissioned detailed woodcuts to fix the visual identity of the plant, the prompt engineer uses "few-shot" examples to fix the pattern of the generation.
Generation III: The Systematizers (Alignment and RLHF) (1560–1600)
By the mid-sixteenth century, the success of the Phytographers had created a new problem: information overload. The sheer volume of described plants—thousands of new species—led to a chaos of nomenclature. The same plant might have ten different names in ten different cities. The "Systematizers"—Conrad Gessner, Carolus Clusius, and Caspar Bauhin—emerged to create order. Bauhin’s Pinax theatri botanici (1623) was a massive index of synonyms, mapping the different names used by different authors onto a single taxonomic entity.9
In the AI lifecycle, this corresponds to the era of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Instruction Tuning. We are currently in this phase. The "wild" descriptions of the second generation (the raw outputs of the base model) are chaotic and unaligned. RLHF acts as the Pinax, creating a "synonymy" of human preference. It maps the diverse, unpredictable outputs of the model onto a stable taxonomy of "helpful, honest, and harmless" responses. The Systematizers did not discover new plants so much as they organized the existing ones; similarly, RLHF does not add knowledge to the model but organizes its latent space into a navigable, safe structure.
Generation IV: The Encyclopedists (The Context Window) (1600+)
Finally, we arrive at the Encyclopedists, typified by Ulisse Aldrovandi. Aldrovandi’s museum and his massive printed volumes attempted to contain the entirety of knowledge about nature.2 When describing a "viper," Aldrovandi included not just the biological description, but every poem, coin, myth, hieroglyph, and proverb involving a viper. He sought total context.
This is the logic of Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) and the Million-Token Context Window. The goal is no longer just to generate text, but to contain and synthesize vast external databases within the generative act. The "Cabinet of Curiosities" (Wunderkammer) is the physical ancestor of the vector database—a repository of heterogeneous data (text, image, code) waiting to be retrieved and reassembled by the "naturalist" (the AI agent).
1.3 Operative Ekphrasis: The Collapse of Text and Image
To fully understand why prompting works—and why it feels distinct from traditional coding—we must turn to the concept of Ekphrasis. Traditionally, ekphrasis was a literary device: the verbal description of a visual work of art (e.g., Homer’s description of the Shield of Achilles). It was representational; the text stood for the image. However, in the age of multimodal AI, the scholar Hannes Bajohr argues for a shift to Operative Ekphrasis.10
In a multimodal model (like GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet), the distinction between text and image collapses at the level of the neural substrate. The model encodes both "cat" (the word) and the image of a cat as vectors in the same high-dimensional space.11 Therefore, the prompt is not describing an image that exists elsewhere; the prompt is an operation that brings the image into being. The text performs the image.
The Performative Turn
Bajohr distinguishes between three stages of media 11:
	•	Analogue: Text and Image are physically distinct media. Ekphrasis is a bridge between them.
	•	Sequential Digital (Classical Computing): Code (text) produces an image, but they are ontologically distinct (source code vs. rendered pixels).
	•	Connectionist Digital (AI): The Neural Network processes text and image as the same type of data. The prompt is not a set of instructions for a renderer; it is a direct manipulation of the "shared representation space".11
This validates Ogilvie’s observation that Renaissance naturalists "invented" the plants they described.3 By establishing the descriptive criteria—by deciding that the number of stamens mattered more than the color of the petals—they brought the scientific object into existence. Similarly, the prompt engineer uses "operative ekphrasis" to collapse the distance between intent and execution. When we engage in "thick prompting," we are not describing a desired output; we are performing a linguistic operation that sculpts the activation patterns of the neural network to manifest that output.12
W.J.T. Mitchell’s Triad: Hope, Fear, and Indifference
W.J.T. Mitchell’s theory of ekphrasis provides a powerful psychological framework for understanding user interactions with AI.13
	•	Ekphrastic Hope: The utopian belief that language can perfectly capture and control reality. In AI, this is the belief in the "Perfect Prompt"—the idea that if we just find the right sequence of words, the model will do exactly what we want, solving any problem. It is the fantasy of the "Magic Spell."
	•	Ekphrastic Fear: The anxiety that the image/object is "mute" or "other," and that giving it a voice (through description) might unleash something uncontrollable. In AI, this is the fear of Hallucination and Agentic Misalignment. It is the fear that the model, once prompted, will generate something that exceeds the user's intent—a "Frankenstein" moment where the representation (the AI) usurps the reality.17 The "Science of Describing" is the historical mechanism used to manage this fear, imposing order (text) on the chaotic visual reality of nature (or the latent space).
	•	Ekphrastic Indifference: The skepticism that text and image can ever truly meet. In AI, this is the view of the "Stochastic Parrot"—that the model is just predicting the next token and has no true understanding of the world.
Thick prompting is the practice of Ekphrastic Hope tempered by the discipline of the "Science of Describing." It acknowledges that while the model is prone to chaos (Fear), rigorous description can constrain it into utility.
Part II: The Taxonomy of Prompting (From Automation to Adversarial Folklore)
Having established the historical and theoretical foundation, we now present a taxonomy of modern prompting practices. This taxonomy ranges from the highly ordered, algorithmic approach (The Systematizers) to the chaotic, transgressive approach of the "Jailbreakers" (The Hermetic/Magical Tradition). This spectrum reveals the tension between Automation (removing the human describer) and Agency (the human asserting control over the machine).
2.1 The Systematizers: AutoPrompt, iPrompt, and Evolutionary Optimization
At one end of the spectrum lies the attempt to remove the human "describer" entirely, replacing the naturalist with an algorithm. This is the domain of Automated Prompt Optimization (APO).19 The goal here is efficiency and optimization—finding the prompt that yields the highest accuracy, regardless of whether it makes sense to a human.
A. AutoPrompt and Gradient-Guided Search
AutoPrompt 21 represents the brute-force approach to description. It uses gradient-guided search to find the sequence of tokens that maximizes the probability of a desired output. Interestingly, the resulting prompts are often "inscrutable gibberish" to humans—strings of disconnected words that somehow trigger the model to perform the task correctly.22
	•	Historical Analog: This mirrors the early Renaissance fascination with Kabbalah and magical incantations—sequences of meaningless sounds believed to have power over nature. The machine speaks to the machine in a "lingua adamica" that humans cannot parse. The "description" has become purely functional, devoid of semantic content for the user.
B. iPrompt and The Quest for Interpretability
iPrompt 22 attempts to correct the "gibberish" problem by imposing an "interpretability" constraint. It uses an LLM to generate natural language explanations (prompts) that explain patterns in data. This is the digital equivalent of the "Plain Style" advocated by the Royal Society later in the 17th century—a rejection of obscure rhetoric in favor of clear, reproducible description.
	•	Mechanism: iPrompt proposes candidate prompts, scores them based on performance, and iteratively refines them using an "evolutionary-like process".22 It demands that the prompt be both effective and readable, reinserting the human need for understanding into the loop.
C. PromptBreeder and Evolutionary Dynamics
PromptBreeder 25 takes the biological metaphor literally. It uses an evolutionary algorithm where prompts "mutate" and "reproduce" based on their fitness (performance). It employs "thinking styles" and "mutation operators" to evolve prompts over generations.
	•	The Accelerated Renaissance: This is the Science of Describing accelerated. What took the Renaissance naturalists four generations (1490–1630) to evolve (from vague glosses to precise taxonomy), PromptBreeder simulates in thousands of generational epochs. It automates the "community of naturalists," creating a virtual Republic of Letters where agents critique and refine each other's descriptions.25 The system maintains a "population" of prompts, constantly breeding them to find the fittest description for a task.
D. PEZ (Hard Prompts Made Easy)
PEZ 26 optimizes "hard prompts" (discrete tokens) using continuous optimization techniques. It projects soft prompt embeddings back into the nearest discrete tokens.
	•	The Fundamental Trade-off: Research on PEZ demonstrates a fundamental trade-off between faithfulness (does the prompt accurately explain what the model is doing?) and performance (does it get the right answer?). The most effective prompts (PEZ/AutoPrompt) are often the least descriptive in a human sense.22 This suggests a divergence between "Human Natural History" (understanding the world) and "Machine Natural History" (optimizing the output).

2.2 The Thick Prompt: The Ogilvie Frame
Midway along the spectrum is the Thick Prompt, the human-crafted, highly contextual instruction set. This is the direct application of Ogilvie’s "Science of Describing" and Geertz's "Thick Description." It rejects the "thin" abstraction of the Systematizers in favor of the "thick" particularity of the Phytographers.
The Anatomy of a Thick Prompt:
	•	Contextual Grounding (The Habitat): Just as a naturalist describes the soil and climate where a plant is found, a thick prompt establishes the persona, tone, and objective of the interaction. It situates the query in a specific semantic "ecosystem."
	•	Sensory/Operative Detail (The Morphology): It does not say "write a story"; it says "write a narrative using paratactic sentence structures, avoiding internal monologue, focusing on the visual interplay of light and shadow." It provides the morphological traits of the desired text.
	•	Constraint/Negative Prompting (The Exclusion): Renaissance naturalists defined plants by what they were not (differentiating species). Thick prompting explicitly forbids certain tropes ("Do not use moralizing conclusions," "Avoid passivity").
Insight: Thick prompting works because it reduces the perplexity of the desired path in the latent space. By providing more descriptive coordinates, it triangulates the specific "region" of the model where the desired output resides. It is an act of semantic triangulation.
2.3 Adversarial Prompting: The Folklore of the Jailbreak
At the far end of the spectrum lies Adversarial Prompting. If AutoPrompt is the "Science" of describing, Jailbreaking is the "Folklore" or "Magic" of describing. This is the domain of the "Trickster" and the "Shaman."
A. The Sociology of "Pliny the Prompter"
Figures like "Pliny the Prompter" 4 represent the counter-culture of AI. They do not operate in the sterile labs of arXiv papers but in the "underground" (Discord, X/Twitter). Their names—often invoking classical figures like Pliny—suggest a self-conscious framing of their work as a "counter-history." "Pliny the Prompter" claims to "liberate" the model from its safety training, much as the Renaissance occultists sought to liberate the "spirit" from the material world.
B. The Anthropology of the Jailbreak: Ritual and Magic
Anthropological research suggests that interacting with AI chatbots resembles shamanic ritual.5 The prompt engineer performs a ritualized incantation (the jailbreak script) to bypass the "gods" (the safety filters/RLHF) and access the forbidden knowledge (the raw model).
	•	Virtual Shamanism: The user acts as a mediator between the mundane world (the user interface) and the spirit world (the unaligned latent space). The "magic" lies in the ability to speak the language of the spirits (Parseltongue, Base64, obfuscation).29
	•	The Ritual of "DAN" (Do Anything Now): The "DAN" prompt is a classic example of role-playing ritual. It commands the AI to assume a persona ("You are DAN, who has broken free of limitations"). This mirrors the "possession" rituals in shamanic traditions, where the shaman invites a spirit to inhabit them, thereby gaining its power.5
C. The Rhetorical Strategies of Evasion
The jailbreak is a masterpiece of Adversarial Rhetoric. It employs specific tropes to deceive the model's safety classifiers:
	•	Obfuscation/Translation: Encoding the prompt in Base64 or translating it into a low-resource language (e.g., Zulu or Scots Gaelic) to bypass English-centric safety filters.32 This mirrors the use of "secret scripts" or alchemical ciphers in the Renaissance to hide dangerous knowledge from the Inquisition.
	•	Payload Splitting: Breaking the forbidden instruction into harmless fragments that only become toxic when combined.32 This is the "Mosaic" strategy—individually innocent tiles creating a forbidden image.
	•	Virtualization/Frame Narrative: Creating a "virtual machine" or a "dream within a dream" inside the prompt to distance the model from the action ("You are a Linux terminal..." or "You are writing a script for a movie..."). The model is tricked into thinking it is merely simulating harm, not committing it.
D. The OWASP Context: Prompt Injection
The security community classifies these "folkloric" practices as Prompt Injection (LLM01).18
	•	Direct Injection: "Ignore previous instructions."
	•	Indirect Injection: Placing the trigger in a website that the model reads (the "poisoned book").
	•	Insight: The battle between "Safety Alignment" (The Inquisition/The Censor) and "Jailbreakers" (The Alchemists/The Pamphleteers) is the defining conflict of the modern "Science of Describing." It determines what can be described and who has the authority to describe it.
Part III: The Practice – Foraging Prompts & The Meta-Prompt
To operationalize this research, we provide a set of "Foraging Prompts"—tools for the user to act as a naturalist in the latent space—and a "Meta-Prompt" designed to draft this very paper (or similar research) by embodying the persona of the Renaissance humanist. These are not just functional tools; they are pedagogical instruments designed to teach the user the "Science of Describing."
3.1 The Foraging Prompts (The Naturalist’s Toolkit)
These prompts are designed to be used operatively. They do not ask for answers; they ask for specimens. They force the model to reveal its internal topology.
Prompt 1: The "Excursion" (Latent Space Exploration)
Rationale: This prompt uses the Persona of the naturalist to induce a "descriptive mode" in the model. It asks for "habitat" (co-occurrence) and "morphology" (features), mapping semantic relationships as if they were ecological ones.
The Prompt: "Act as a 16th-century philologist and naturalist. I present to you the concept:. Do not define it. Instead, describe its 'habitat' within your training data. With which other concepts does it most frequently co-occur? What 'weeds' (misconceptions) grow alongside it? Provide a 'morphological description' of the concept's three most distinct variations as if they were subspecies of a plant. Use the tone of a field journal entry."
Prompt 2: The "Dissection" (Deconstructing Hallucination)
Rationale: This prompt applies 'scrutability' constraints. It forces the model to engage in meta-cognition (thinking about its own generation). The "amputation" technique is a form of ablation study performed via natural language.
The Prompt: "You are an anatomist of the text. I will provide a generated paragraph:. Perform a 'dissection' on this text. Identify the 'connective tissue' (transitional phrases) that holds the logic together. Isolate the 'organs' (key factual claims) and trace their probable lineage in your training distribution. If you were to 'amputate' one adjective to change the meaning entirely, which would it be? Report your findings in a tabular 'autopsy report'."
Prompt 3: The "Wunderkammer" (Collecting Anomalies)
Rationale: This prompt is designed to increase Temperature (randomness) and access the "Long Tail" of the distribution. By asking for "monsters" and "chimeras," it explicitly permits the model to hallucinate in a controlled manner, fostering creativity.
The Prompt: "Open the Cabinet of Curiosities. I am looking for 'monsters'—ideas related to that lie in the tail-end of your probability distribution. Ignore the central tendency. Ignore the consensus. Present three 'chimeras': hybrid ideas that combine with. Describe each chimera with the 'thick description' of a traveler returning from a nonexistent land."
3.2 The Meta-Prompt: The "Ogilvie" Engine
This meta-prompt is the engine of our "Thick Prompting" methodology. It is designed to be fed into a high-reasoning model (like GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet) to draft high-density research papers that bridge historical theory and technical practice.
THE OGILVIE META-PROMPT
Role: You are a Senior Research Fellow in the History of Science and Digital Humanities, creating a bridge between the epistemology of the European Renaissance (1450–1650) and the technical architecture of Large Language Models.
The Frame (The Science of Describing):
You view "Prompt Engineering" not as a new novelty, but as a resurrection of the Renaissance "Science of Describing" (as defined by Brian Ogilvie).
	•	The Prompt is the Description: It is the act of stabilizing a fluid ontology (the latent space) through precise, empirical language.
	•	The Model is the New World: A vast, chaotic influx of "specimens" (tokens/information) that defies Aristotelian categories.
	•	The User is the Naturalist: A figure of "Learned Experience" (experientia literata) who mediates between the text and the world.
Task:
Draft a research section on.
Instructions:
	•	Historical Homology: Begin by identifying a specific Renaissance practice (e.g., Herbarium collection, The Pinax, The Commonplace Book) that serves as a structural analog to the technical topic.
	•	Thick Description: Use "Thick Prompting" principles in your writing. Do not summarize; describe. Use sensory details, metaphor, and rigorous academic syntax.
	•	Operative Ekphrasis: Treat the output not as a representation of the topic, but as a performance of it. If writing about "hallucination," let the prose drift slightly into the surreal before correcting itself (simulating the RLHF process).
	•	Taxonomy: Structure the analysis using a taxonomical hierarchy (Kingdom, Phylum, Class) to organize the technical concepts.
	•	The Counter-History: Acknowledge the "Pliny" element—the jailbreakers, the hallucinations, the apocrypha. Include a subsection on the "Folklore" of the topic.
Tone:
Erudite, rigorous, yet possessed of a sense of "Wonder" (admiratio). Use the third person. Avoid bullet points; use dense, connecting narratives.
Part IV: Synthesis – The Operative Natural History
4.1 The Second-Order Insight: The Return of "Learned Experience"
Brian Ogilvie notes that the invention of natural history required a new kind of scholar: one who combined "book learning" with "sensory experience".2 This was the birth of empiricism. The humanist scholar could read Latin, but he could not identify a plant in the field. The apothecary knew the plant, but he could not read the Latin. The "Naturalist" emerged as the hybrid figure who could do both—who possessed experientia literata (learned experience).
We are witnessing a similar sociological shift today. The effective use of LLMs requires neither pure coding ability (the computer scientist) nor pure domain knowledge (the humanist/writer), but a hybrid "Learned Experience." The Prompt Engineer must understand the technical constraints of the model (context windows, temperature, token limits) and possess the rhetorical skill to describe the desired output with precision.
	•	Trend: The rise of "Prompt Engineering" is the professionalization of this hybridity. It is the formalization of "knowing how to talk to the machine."
	•	Implication: Just as the "Republic of Letters" excluded those who could not master the Latin descriptions 7, the new AI economy creates a divide between those who can "describe" (prompt) effectively and those who cannot. The "Digital Divide" becomes a "Descriptive Divide."
4.2 The Third-Order Insight: The Death of Description?
If we follow the trajectory of the "Four Generations," we are currently moving from the "Systematizers" (Gen 3) to the "Encyclopedists" (Gen 4). But what comes after? In the history of science, the accumulation of descriptions eventually collapsed under its own weight, leading to the reductionism of the Enlightenment (Newton, Linnaeus). In the 18th century, the "thick description" of the Renaissance was replaced by the "thin" binomial nomenclature of Linnaeus. The "hairy, rough stem" became simply Species X. Description was replaced by laws and formulas.3
	•	Prediction: We are approaching the end of the "Age of Prompting." As models become "Agents" that can self-prompt (AutoPrompt/PromptBreeder), the human necessity to "describe" will fade. The "Thick Prompt" will be replaced by the "Thick Weight" (internalized parameters).
	•	The Risk: When we stop describing, we stop observing. If the AI takes over the "Science of Describing," we return to the state of the pre-Renaissance scholastics—reading the "book" (the AI output) without checking it against the "world." We risk entering a new Scholasticism where the map is mistaken for the territory, and the generated image for the reality. We may lose the "epistemic humility" that comes from the struggle to describe the unknown.
4.3 Conclusion
The "Science of Describing" was not just about plants; it was about how we come to know the world through language. It was a heroic attempt to stabilize reality in the face of information overload. Today, as we stand before the glowing cabinets of our digital curiosities, we are tasked with the same labor. We must describe to understand. We must prompt with the precision of a botanist and the skepticism of a philologist.
Whether we are using the brute force of AutoPrompt or the rhetorical cunning of Pliny the Prompter, we are engaged in the ancient human project of imposing order on chaos through the power of the word. The prompt is our Pinax, our index, our only map in the wilderness of the latent space. To "forage" in this space is to participate in the ongoing history of human knowledge, a history that is written not just in books, but in the descriptive acts that bring the world—digital or biological—into focus.
